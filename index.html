The short version

Four townhomes in your subdivision have sold in 2026. They tell a consistent and unusually clear story, and it comes down to one variable.

Condition is setting price in this subdivision — not size, not location, not timing. Two units of identical square footage sold five months apart for a $55,000 difference. The only meaningful difference between them was that one had been updated and one had not.

At the same time, a 3,300-home master-planned community is now selling brand-new townhomes roughly a mile from your front door, in your same ZIP code, with builder incentives no individual seller can match. That competition is not a passing condition. It is a multi-year supply pipeline.

The practical conclusion: this property will sell, and it can sell well. But it will sell either updated and patient, or discounted and fast. The middle path — listing it as-is at an updated price and waiting for the market to agree — is the one the data most clearly rules out.

Why the price gap is smaller than it looks. A buyer comparing your property at $330,000 to a new townhome at $395,000 is not comparing a $65,000 difference. They are comparing monthly payments — and a builder rate buydown can close most of that gap. That is the competitive reality, and it is also the reason a dated resale struggles: once the payments are close, the buyer takes the new one.

What actually reaches you at closing

Sale price is not proceeds. Two categories reduce it, and the second one surprises most long-term rental owners.

Transaction costs

Real estate commission, Florida documentary stamp tax on the deed, title and closing fees, and prorated taxes and association dues. On a sale in this price range these typically total in the range of 7% to 9% of the sale price, depending on the commission structure and what is negotiated with the buyer.

Taxes — and this is the one to plan for

Because this property has been held as a rental, the tax treatment is different from selling a primary residence, and it involves two separate calculations:

  • Capital gains on the appreciation since you purchased in 2011. Held this long, this is a long-term gain. Florida imposes no state income tax, so this is a federal calculation.
  • Depreciation recapture. Every year the property has been a rental, a portion of its value has been deducted as depreciation. When you sell, the IRS recaptures that benefit — taxed at a federal rate of up to 25%, separately from and in addition to capital gains. This applies to depreciation you were entitled to take, whether or not it was actually claimed.

Over roughly fifteen years of rental ownership, recapture is not a rounding error. It is a specific number, it is calculable in advance, and it should not be discovered at tax time.

The primary-residence exclusion that shelters up to $250,000 of gain generally does not apply to a property that has been a rental rather than your home. If deferring the tax is a priority, a 1031 exchange — rolling the proceeds into another investment property — is the mechanism worth discussing, but it has strict deadlines that begin at closing and must be set up before the sale, not after.

Your three realistic paths

Update, then list

  • Scope the work, get bids, complete it
  • List at $339,000–$345,000
  • Expect 50–75 days on market
  • Strongest position against new construction
  • Requires capital up front and a vacancy period

List as-is

Lower cost, lower net

  • No capital outlay
  • List at $315,000, expect reductions
  • Expect 110–190 days on market
  • Every month carries taxes, association dues, insurance and utilities
  • Historically ends near $295,000–$305,000

Sell to an investor

Fastest — and the most expensive

  • Cash, as-is, no repairs, quick close
  • Investors price in the same work plus a profit margin
  • Expect meaningfully below the as-is retail range
  • Appropriate if speed and certainty outweigh price
  • Worth knowing the number even if you don't take it
The one path the data does not support is listing the property in current condition at an updated price and waiting. That is precisely what happened at 9714 Tiramasu Trail: it opened at $325,000, spent roughly six months on the market through a long series of reductions, and closed at $260,000. Time on the market is not neutral. Buyers read it, and it costs more than the reduction itself.
